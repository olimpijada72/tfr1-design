    The TfR1 Plan

    One GPU, two hundred dollars, eight days around a full-time job, and twenty submission slots. This page proposes a pipeline that fits inside all four constraints, and argues for the one architectural decision everything else follows from.

    Companion to  The TfR1 Problem Written  Sat 24 October 2026 Deadline  Sun 1 November 2026
    00

    The plan on one screen

    The primer established what we are building and why it is hard. This page is about how to actually get it done in the time and money available, and the honest answer is that the constraints do most of the design work for us.

    ConstraintValueWhat it forces
    Compute~$200One mid-range GPU, roughly 100 rentable hours. One generation run, not three.
    Calendar8 daysNo tool we have not already used. Setup failures are the main risk, not model quality.
    AttentionEvenings + 3 weekend daysLong jobs must run unattended overnight. The GPU works while we don't.
    Submissions20Selection matters as much as generation. The last mile is a portfolio problem.

    The whole plan in one sentence: spend the money generating a large pool of binders that ignore pH entirely, then install the pH switch afterwards by hand, on the laptop, for free.

    Everything below is an elaboration of that sentence. Section 01 argues for it, section 02 lays out the pipeline it implies, sections 03 to 08 specify each stage, and sections 09 and 10 cover the money and the calendar.

    01

    Where the pH switch gets installed

    The primer's closing observation is the thing that shapes this entire architecture, so it is worth restating in engineering terms. No model in the toolchain has any representation of pH. RFdiffusion, AlphaFold and ProteinMPNN all see a histidine as simply another residue type. None of them knows it is neutral at 7.4 and positive at 6.0, and none of them can be asked.

    So requirement ③ cannot emerge from the generative step. It has to be installed somewhere, deliberately, by us. There are exactly three places it could go.

    OptionMechanismWhy / why not
    A  Bias generationPush the designer toward histidine-rich interfacesFights the model's learned preferences and costs affinity — the requirement we can least afford to lose. Reject.
    B  Graft afterwardsDesign pH-blind, then mutate interface positions to histidine and re-scoreCheap, controllable, and when a histidine wrecks the interface we can see it in the re-folded structure. Adopt.
    C  Spread the betUse the 20 slots to cover a range of histidine placementsThe switch is unmeasurable in silico. If it cannot be scored, it must be sampled. Adopt.
    Consequence for scope
    Do not set out to generate twenty designs

    Generate 50–100 good binders. Expand each into a small family of histidine variants. Cut back to twenty at the very end.

    The funnel should be at its widest after generation, not before it. This is the single most important number in the plan, because it sets how much GPU time the generation stage gets.

    03

    Stage 1 — Target prep

    This stage costs nothing and takes an evening, and it is the highest-leverage thing in the project. Get it wrong and every dollar downstream is wasted on the wrong problem.

    Start from 1CX8 in the Protein Data Bank — the crystal structure of the human TfR1 ectodomain. Then throw most of it away.

    Concretely:

    • Cut to residues ~189–383. Confirm the boundaries in PyMOL rather than trusting the number — a cut through the middle of a helix will produce a frayed, unphysical edge that the designer will happily bind to.
    • Reduce to one chain. TfR1 is a homodimer, but a single apical domain is the design surface. The second copy doubles cost for nothing.
    • Strip waters, sugars, ions and other heteroatoms. Leftover glycans are a classic way to silently poison a run.
    • Note the three conserved positions identified in the primer. These become the hotspot residues passed to the generator.

    Mouse needs a sequence, not a structure

    Mouse TfR1 is Q62351 in UniProt. Because mouse is only used for filtering in stage 4, and the structure predictor builds its own model from sequence, no experimental mouse structure is required. Align the mouse sequence to human, take the span corresponding to the apical domain just carved out, and set it aside.

    Done when
    • A single-chain PDB file of ~195 residues, visually inspected, no heteroatoms.
    • A mouse apical-domain sequence in FASTA, aligned and span-matched.
    • Three hotspot residue numbers written down.

    Stage 2 — Generation

    This is the one expensive stage, and there are two realistic ways to run it.

    BindCraftRFdiffusion → MPNN → AF2

    Decision
    BindCraft

    The DIY route is genuinely cheaper per design and gives finer control. It is still the wrong trade here. With eight days and a day job, what sinks the project is not a mediocre hit rate — it is losing Tuesday evening to a CUDA version conflict.

    Keep RFdiffusion in reserve, and reach for it only if BindCraft turns out to produce almost nothing on this epitope after the Sunday pilot.

    The one parameter worth arguing about

    Set the binder length to roughly 65–90 residues, rather than taking the default.

    The primer already made this argument in its discussion of the narrow viable band. A large binder buries a lot of surface and spreads its binding energy thinly across hundreds of small contacts, and an interface built that way cannot be broken by one protonated histidine — it lands in the "too well buried" zone and fails requirement ③. A short binder is forced to concentrate its grip, which is exactly the single-point-of-failure property the switch depends on.

    We are deliberately not maximising affinity. Every design choice in this stage trades a little stickiness for a concentrated interface, because stickiness we can measure and the switch we cannot.

    05

    Stages 3 and 4 — The filters

    Two cuts, both cheap in GPU terms, applied in order of increasing cost.

    Affinity — rescore with ipSAE, not ipTM

    The default confidence score for a predicted complex is ipTM, and it has a specific failure mode here. It is computed across the whole system, so with a ~195-residue target and a ~75-residue binder, most of the number reflects how well AlphaFold understands the target — something we already know and do not care about. The interface we actually designed barely moves it.

    ipSAE, a single Python script from the Dunbrack lab, rescores the same AlphaFold output focusing on inter-chain contacts. It costs nothing, takes an afternoon to wire in, and separates designs far better than ipTM does.

    Cross-species — the brutal cut

    Take everything that survived and re-predict it against the mouse apical sequence instead of the human one, using ColabFold (a packaged local AlphaFold that handles the database lookups) or Boltz-2 (a newer open model that predicts complexes directly). Keep only designs confident against both species.

    Expect attrition

    This will remove half to three quarters of the pool. That is not a failure of the stage — it is the reason the plan over-generates in the first place. A pool of 20 entering stage 4 leaves nothing to work with; a pool of 100 leaves about 30.

    06

    Stage 5 — pH engineering

    This runs entirely on the laptop, costs nothing, and is the only stage where our own reasoning beats the defaults. It is also where the competition is actually won or lost.

    Choosing where the histidine goes

    For each surviving design, list the residues within about 5 Å of the target — those are the ones making contact. Then flag the subset sitting beside an arginine or a lysine, on either side of the interface, since both stay positive at both pH values.

    This is the placement the primer singles out, and the logic is worth restating. A histidine does the most damage when it acquires its charge directly next to another positive charge, because we are not merely deleting a favourable contact — we are creating an actively repulsive one.

    Build a family, then score it

    From each design, generate a small set of variants — one single histidine at the best-looking position, a couple of doubles at genuinely distinct sites, and one aggressive triple. Then score with free CPU tools.

    ToolWhat it givesHow to read it
    PROPKA3Predicted pKa of each ionisable residue, from a structureThe useful quantity is the shift between bound complex and free binder. A histidine whose pKa is pushed down by burial is a loaded spring — it wants a proton and cannot have one while bound.
    FoldXBinding energy at a specified pH (free academic licence)Take the difference between its answers at 7.4 and 6.0. Rank by that gap.
    Rosetta pH_modeThe same idea, inside one ecosystemAn alternative to FoldX, not an addition to it. Pick one.
    ColabFold re-foldStructure of each variantThe pass/fail gate. Confirms the histidine has not simply destroyed the interface — which happens often.

    Be honest about what these numbers are worth. Neither PROPKA nor FoldX was built for designed interfaces, and published accuracy on them is poor. Use them to rank-order candidates that are already structurally sound. Never use them to rescue a design that failed stage 3 or 4.

    07

    Stage 6 — The twenty slots

    Treat this as portfolio construction, not as taking the top twenty rows of a spreadsheet.

    The reasoning is straightforward. If the histidine hypothesis is wrong, a submission ranked purely by predicted affinity fails uniformly and teaches us nothing. A deliberate spread fails partially and tells us which direction to move. Given that requirement ③ is unmeasurable in silico, a spread is the only design that returns information either way.

    SlotsDesign classWhat it tests
    6Highest affinity, single well-placed HisThe conservative bet: minimum perturbation to a strong interface.
    8Mid affinity, two His at distinct positionsThe central hypothesis. Two shots at a switch without over-committing.
    4Aggressive, three HisCovers the case where one protonation is simply not enough force.
    2Unmodified parents, no His addedInternal control. Separates "the binder was bad" from "the His broke it".

    Those last two slots feel like a waste of submissions and are not. Without them, a uniformly negative result is uninterpretable.

    Diversity and novelty

    • No pair above ~60% sequence identity, and no two submissions using the same fold against the same patch. Twenty near-copies is one experiment, not twenty.
    • Foldseek every finalist against the whole PDB. Foldseek is a fast structural search tool; the rules require de novo designs, and we want that evidence collected calmly on Saturday rather than scrambled at the deadline.
    • Inspect all twenty by eye in PyMOL. Twenty structures is twenty minutes, and it catches the things no metric reports — a binder threaded through a loop, an interface that is all backbone, a histidine pointing into solvent.
    08

    The money

    Rent an L40S, not an A100. Roughly $1.50–2/hr against about $3, and with the target truncated to ~195 residues the job fits comfortably in 48 GB. The extra A100 capacity buys nothing; the lower price buys half again as many hours.

    Call it ~100 hours available. Plan to spend 50–60 and hold the rest back, because some will be wasted and it is better to know that in advance.

    Three habits that matter more than they sound

    HabitWhy
    Stop the instance whenever you walk awayOne idle GPU overnight is ~$20 — 10% of the budget, spent on nothing. This is the most common way credits disappear.
    Everything on a persistent volumeSo that stopping the instance does not mean reinstalling it. Set this up on day one, before anything else.
    Run stages 1, 5 and 6 locallyNone of them need a GPU, and together they are most of the actual working hours.
    09

    The eight days

    The calendar has an unusual shape this time: the weekend is at the front, not the end. Today is Saturday. That is lucky, because setup and target prep are the two tasks that most need an uninterrupted block, and they are also the two that must happen first.

    The structural trick of the week: the GPU runs while you are at work, and you work while the GPU would otherwise be idle. Every overnight run not launched is a day that cannot be recovered.

    Sunday go/no-go
    • Are designs passing BindCraft's own filters at a usable rate, or near zero?
    • Are they landing on the apical patch, and nowhere near 619–670?
    • If both are bad: switch to the RFdiffusion route on Sunday, not Wednesday. After Sunday there is no time to change engines.

    The honest caveat

    The pH switch is a coin flip that cannot be de-risked computationally with the tools that exist today. This plan maximises the number of well-folded, cross-reactive, high-affinity designs submitted, and spreads the histidine bets across them.

    Go in expecting that requirements ① and ② are what we are engineering, and requirement ③ is what we are sampling. Any plan that claims otherwise is overselling what the toolchain can do.

    That framing also sets what counts as success. Twenty designs that bind both species tightly, carrying a deliberate and documented spread of histidine placements, is a good submission even if none of them switch — because it is the version of this experiment that produces an interpretable result.

    Immediate next step. Stage 1 is free, needs no GPU, and blocks everything else. The target-prep script — truncate 1CX8, strip heteroatoms, extract and align the mouse span — is the right thing to write first, today.

    Cost figures are current GPU rental rates and should be confirmed against live pricing before committing. Residue ranges follow the primer and are worth re-checking against the structure itself.
